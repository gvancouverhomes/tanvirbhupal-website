I get this question all the time. A buyer walks into a townhouse showing, sees the strata fee, and makes a snap judgment. Lower fee, better deal. Higher fee, bad deal. That's backwards. I've watched too many buyers get burned by this thinking.

A fee doesn't mean much on its own. It only makes sense next to the building's age, its amenities, what the fee includes, and how well the strata is planning for maintenance and future repairs. There is no single "normal" Fleetwood townhouse strata fee that tells you whether a complex is well run.

## The Low Fee Trap

A low fee feels like a win. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it is a reason to look closer.

Compare fees across Fleetwood townhouse complexes and you will see differences. If one fee looks much lower than comparable properties, don't assume you found a bargain. Find out why.

Strata fees fund the corporation's shared expenses. In B.C., that includes contributions to both the operating fund and the Contingency Reserve Fund, commonly called the CRF. The operating fund handles expenses that usually occur at least annually, while the CRF is intended for expenses that happen less frequently, such as replacing a roof or repaving a private road. The Province explains the distinction in its [guide to budgeting and strata fees](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/housing-tenancy/strata-housing/operating-a-strata/finances-and-insurance/budgeting-and-strata-fees?keyword=0){: target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" }.

If a strata keeps contributions too low for the work its property will eventually need, the bill doesn't disappear. It waits. Then one day the roof needs replacing, the building exterior needs major work, or roads and common property need significant repairs. If the available funds are not enough, owners can face a special levy.

That's why I would rather understand *why* a fee is low than celebrate the number on the listing.

## What a Fair Fee Actually Pays For

A well-run complex should have a budget that reflects what the property genuinely costs to operate and maintain. Depending on the development, strata expenses can include things such as:

- strata corporation insurance
- landscaping and common-area maintenance
- utilities paid by the strata
- property management and professional services
- routine repairs and maintenance
- contributions to the Contingency Reserve Fund

What is included varies from one complex to another, which is why comparing two monthly fees without comparing the budgets can be misleading.

Under current B.C. rules, a strata corporation must contribute at least 10% of the amount budgeted for the operating fund to its CRF each year, and CRF contributions are supposed to be considered alongside the depreciation report. That minimum does not tell you whether the fund is adequate for a particular property. The repair schedule, current reserve balance and expected future costs still matter.

This is one reason the age of the complex matters so much. If you are deciding between different generations of Fleetwood townhouse developments, my guide to [older Fleetwood townhouses vs. new builds]({{ '/articles/older-fleetwood-townhouses-vs-new-builds-which-offers-better-value/' | relative_url }}) looks at the broader trade-off between newer systems and finishes versus the space, storage and layouts you may find in established complexes.

## Read the Paperwork Before You Judge the Number

You can't tell if a fee is appropriate just by looking at it. You have to dig into the documents behind it.

### The depreciation report

This is one of the most useful documents in the package because it looks at the common property and major assets, estimates maintenance and replacement costs over time, and provides long-term funding information.

B.C. now generally requires strata corporations with five or more lots to obtain a depreciation report on a five-year cycle. For existing strata corporations in Metro Vancouver that did not already have a sufficiently recent report, the strengthened provincial requirements set a July 1, 2026 deadline. You can read the Province's current [strata depreciation report requirements](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/housing-tenancy/strata-housing/operating-a-strata/repairs-and-maintenance/depreciation-reports/depreciation-report-requirements){: target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" }.

The report matters because today's monthly fee has to be considered against tomorrow's work. A low fee is less impressive if major expenses are approaching and the funding plan is weak.

### Council meeting minutes

These tell you the real story.

Is the council addressing small problems before they become large ones? Are owners repeatedly arguing about the same repair? Has parking become an ongoing issue? Are there discussions about leaks, drainage, roofs, insurance or upcoming projects?

The minutes can also reveal practical problems that have nothing to do with the condition of the building. Visitor parking is a good example. If parking matters to your household, my guide to [visitor parking in Fleetwood strata complexes]({{ '/articles/the-visitor-parking-problem-in-fleetwood-strata-complexes-how-to-avoid-it/' | relative_url }}) explains what I would look for beyond the site plan.

### The operating budget and CRF

The budget shows where the money is going. I want to see whether the expenses make sense for the property and whether the strata's financial planning lines up with what the depreciation report says is coming.

The CRF balance matters too, but the balance by itself is not enough. A large fund can still be inadequate if expensive work is approaching. A smaller fund may make more sense in a newer or simpler complex with fewer near-term projects. Context matters.

## The Monthly Fee Is Only One Part of the Townhouse

This is where buyers sometimes get tunnel vision.

You can spend so much time comparing whether one property is $50 or $100 cheaper per month that you miss the things you will notice every single day: parking, stairs, storage, outdoor space, room sizes and the way the complex is laid out.

For example, if your household has two vehicles, the difference between a tandem garage and a true side-by-side garage may matter more to you than a modest difference in strata fees. My guide to [Fleetwood townhomes with true side-by-side garages]({{ '/articles/where-to-find-fleetwood-townhomes-with-true-side-by-side-garages/' | relative_url }}) goes deeper into that issue.

The same applies when comparing a specific newer community. A newer complex may have newer building systems and finishes, but that does not automatically tell you whether the layout, parking or monthly costs fit your household. My [review of the Wood & Water community by Anthem Properties]({{ '/articles/an-honest-review-of-the-wood-and-water-community-by-anthem-properties/' | relative_url }}) looks at those day-to-day considerations in more detail.

## Cheap Isn't the Question. Appropriate Is.

Stop asking if the fee is cheap. Ask if it's appropriate.

A townhouse with a slightly higher fee may be the smarter buy if the property is being maintained properly, the budget is realistic, the reserve planning makes sense and major projects are being dealt with responsibly. But the higher fee itself does not prove any of that. You still have to read the documents.

A cheap fee with a neglected building is a ticking clock. A higher fee with poor planning isn't much better.

The number only becomes useful once you understand what you're paying for and what the strata is preparing for next.

## Frequently Asked Questions

<div class="faq-list">
  <details>
    <summary>Does a higher strata fee mean the complex is well-managed?</summary>
    <p>No. A higher fee can reflect insurance, utilities, amenities, maintenance requirements, CRF contributions or other operating costs. You still need to review the budget, depreciation report, CRF balance and meeting minutes to understand whether the money is being managed responsibly.</p>
  </details>

  <details>
    <summary>How often do strata fees go up?</summary>
    <p>Strata fees are tied to the annual budget approved by owners, so they can change from year to year as operating costs and funding needs change. Insurance, utilities, maintenance and future repair planning can all affect the amount. I would be more interested in whether the budget is realistic than whether the fee stayed unchanged.</p>
  </details>

  <details>
    <summary>Do amenities justify a higher strata fee?</summary>
    <p>They can. Clubhouses, gyms, pools and other shared facilities can create additional operating, insurance and maintenance costs. Whether those costs are worthwhile depends on how much value those amenities add to your household and how well they are maintained.</p>
  </details>

  <details>
    <summary>What's the best way to compare fees between two Fleetwood townhouse complexes?</summary>
    <p>Don't compare the monthly dollar amount alone. Look at what each fee includes, the size and unit entitlement of the strata lot, the age and complexity of the development, the operating budget, CRF contributions, depreciation report, maintenance history and upcoming projects. Two similar-looking townhouses can have very different financial responsibilities.</p>
  </details>

  <details>
    <summary>What if the contingency reserve fund is nearly empty?</summary>
    <p>Find out why before drawing a conclusion. Review recent expenditures, the depreciation report and any major work already approved or expected. If significant repairs are approaching and available funding is limited, there may be a greater risk of increased fees, borrowing or a special levy.</p>
  </details>
