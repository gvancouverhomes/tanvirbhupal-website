Buyers looking for a townhouse in Fleetwood run into the same question over and over. Do you go modern, or do you go big? A new build gives you sleek finishes and move-in-ready convenience. An older complex gives you space and character. But here's the thing: a home's real value isn't about how it looks. It's about building age, strata health and layout, and how those things shape your daily life and your wallet down the road.

## The Appeal of Modern Infill Developments

Newer developments in Fleetwood can be built for easy living. Open-concept layouts. High-end materials. Smart-home features. Fewer immediate renovation headaches. New homes built by licensed residential builders in B.C. are generally required to carry 2-5-10 home warranty insurance, with coverage for specified defects in materials and labour, the building envelope and structural components. You can review the current [B.C. home warranty requirements](https://www.bchousing.org/licensing-consumer-services/new-homes/home-warranty-insurance-new-homes){: target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" } through BC Housing.

That does not mean every new home is automatically well-built or maintenance-free. Construction quality, design and execution still vary from one project to another.

The other trade-off can be space. Fleetwood's current planning direction concentrates much of its future growth around SkyTrain stations and transit corridors, with higher-density development focused near the station areas. That does not mean every newer townhouse will be smaller, but buyers comparing projects may encounter tighter floor plans, tandem parking or less storage depending on the development. The City of Surrey's [Fleetwood Plan](https://www.surrey.ca/renovating-building-development/land-planning-development/land-use-planning/fleetwood-land-use-plans/fleetwood-town-centre-plan){: target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" } provides the broader planning context.

For a family that needs room for gear, vehicles or a home office, missing space becomes a daily annoyance. No amount of nice finishes fixes that.

If you want to see how those trade-offs can play out in a newer Fleetwood community, my [Wood & Water review]({{ '/articles/an-honest-review-of-the-wood-and-water-community-by-anthem-properties/' | relative_url }}) looks at the actual layout, stairs, parking and living-space considerations.

## The Value Proposition of Established Complexes

Older complexes tell a different story. Look near 168 Street, or the quieter streets around Fleetwood Park. In my experience, established complexes from the 1990s and early 2000s can offer bigger rooms, more generous storage and, in some cases, true side-by-side garages.

For a lot of buyers, that extra space beats fancy finishes every time. A bigger home flexes with your life, whether that's a growing family or a place to store your gear. Established complexes can also have mature landscaping and a quieter feel that is harder to replicate in a brand-new development.

Garage layout is one of those details that can change how useful a townhouse feels every single day. If two-car parking matters, my guide to [Fleetwood townhomes with true side-by-side garages]({{ '/articles/where-to-find-fleetwood-townhomes-with-true-side-by-side-garages/' | relative_url }}) explains why I would verify the actual floor plan instead of relying on the words "double garage."

## Beyond the Sticker Price: Strata Health

Here's where most buyers go wrong. They look at the price tag or the monthly fee and stop there. But the real cost of ownership comes down to something more important: how healthy is the strata corporation?

### The Deferred Maintenance Trap

An older complex might look like a deal because of its lower price. But watch out. A low strata fee isn't always good news. Sometimes it means fees have not kept pace with the property's actual maintenance needs. If major work such as roofing, windows, fencing or paving has been delayed and the available funds are not enough, owners can eventually face a special levy.

That is why I would never judge a townhouse by the monthly number alone. My guide to [Fleetwood townhouse strata fees]({{ '/articles/what-is-a-normal-strata-fee-for-a-fleetwood-townhouse/' | relative_url }}) goes deeper into what the fee is supporting and what I would look for in the financial documents.

### Assessing Management Quality

I've seen it play out both ways. A well-run 25-year-old complex often beats a five-year-old building with a weak contingency reserve fund or a council that just reacts to problems instead of planning ahead. Age tells you one thing. How the strata council runs things, and what the depreciation report says, tells you a lot more about what you'll actually pay down the line.

In B.C., strata corporations with five or more strata lots are now required to obtain depreciation reports on a five-year cycle. The Province explains that these reports help owners plan for the repair and replacement of common property and assets over time and can help reduce the risk of unexpected special levies. You can read the current [B.C. depreciation report requirements](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/housing-tenancy/strata-housing/operating-a-strata/repairs-and-maintenance/depreciation-reports/depreciation-report-requirements){: target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" }.

### The Due Diligence Process

Before you commit to anything, read the council meeting minutes. Check the operating budget. Look at the contingency reserve fund and depreciation report. Find out whether the complex is planning ahead for repairs or mostly responding after problems appear.

The physical condition matters too. If you're buying an older property, my guide to [three things I watch for on Fleetwood home inspections]({{ '/articles/three-things-i-watch-for-on-fleetwood-home-inspections/' | relative_url }}) gives you another way to think about age-related issues and where further investigation may be worth doing.

That's how you protect yourself from a nasty surprise bill later.

## Making the Decision: A Practical Framework

When you're choosing between new and old, think about your actual life, not what's trending right now.

Go new construction if you want newer systems and finishes, fewer immediate renovation projects and the newest look and feel. Check the actual warranty, layout, parking and strata documents rather than assuming new automatically means trouble-free.

Go established if your life needs extra storage, side-by-side parking or bigger rooms, and you're willing to dig into a property's physical and financial health to get that space.

Neither choice wins automatically. The better value is the home that gives you the things you will actually use without hiding costs or compromises you will regret later.

  <details>
    <summary>Is it always a bad sign if a complex has a history of special levies?</summary>
    <p>Not always. Sometimes a special levy means the strata addressed necessary maintenance instead of ignoring it. I would look at what the levy paid for, whether the work was completed properly, what the depreciation report says is coming next, and whether the current budget and reserve contributions look more sustainable going forward.</p>
  </details>

  <details>
    <summary>Can I renovate an older townhome to make it feel more modern?</summary>
    <p>Often, yes. Flooring, cabinets, paint and light fixtures can completely change how an older home feels. But because it is a strata property, check the bylaws and confirm whether strata approval is required before assuming a particular renovation is allowed. The advantage is that you may be able to keep the larger square footage or more practical layout of an older home while updating the interior over time.</p>
  </details>

  <details>
    <summary>What should I look for during an inspection of an older townhome?</summary>
    <p>Pay attention to the bigger-ticket components and systems that could affect the unit or the strata, including windows, building-envelope concerns, heating equipment, hot-water systems and signs of moisture or deferred maintenance. Some common-property items may fall under the strata rather than the individual owner, which is why the inspection and the strata documents should be reviewed together. My guide to <a href="{{ '/articles/three-things-i-watch-for-on-fleetwood-home-inspections/' | relative_url }}">Fleetwood home inspections</a> covers the issue in more detail.</p>
  </details>

  <details>
    <summary>Why do some newer townhomes feel smaller than older ones?</summary>
    <p>There is no rule that newer townhouses must be smaller. In Fleetwood, however, current planning concentrates more housing around future SkyTrain stations and transit corridors, and some newer higher-density projects use land differently than established townhouse complexes. Depending on the development, that can mean different room sizes, parking configurations, storage or outdoor space. Compare the actual floor plan rather than using age alone as a shortcut.</p>
  </details>

  <details>
    <summary>How do I find out if a complex is well-managed?</summary>
    <p>Start with the strata meeting minutes, operating budget, contingency reserve fund and depreciation report. Look for evidence that maintenance is being discussed before it becomes urgent, that major projects are being planned for, and that the strata has a realistic way to fund upcoming work. One document rarely tells the whole story, so I would read them together rather than treating a low fee or a large reserve balance as proof on its own.</p>
  </details>
