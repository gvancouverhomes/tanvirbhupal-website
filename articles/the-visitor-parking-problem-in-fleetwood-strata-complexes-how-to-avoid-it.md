Buying a townhouse or condo in Fleetwood is exciting. You find the layout you love, the price works, and you're ready to sign. But there's one thing a lot of buyers miss until it's too late: visitor parking.

A site plan might show visitor stalls right there in black and white. That doesn't mean they'll be open when your mother-in-law shows up for dinner, or when your caregiver needs to park for a few hours. The paper and the parking lot don't always match.

## The "Available Stall" Myth

Here's the trap. Buyers see visitor parking marked on a plan and assume it's there for them. It isn't always.

Some residents park their extra cars in visitor stalls because they've got nowhere else to put them. Depending on the complex, guests may have to register a vehicle, display a permit, or follow overnight parking procedures. Some complexes technically have visitor stalls, but they're tucked way at the back of the property. Try getting your elderly aunt to walk that far with her bags.

And timing matters. A parking lot that looks wide open on a Tuesday morning can be completely packed by Friday night.

Private parking matters here too. A townhouse with more usable resident parking can reduce some of that pressure, although it does not guarantee that visitor stalls will be easy to find. If parking for two vehicles is important to you, my guide to [Fleetwood townhomes with true side-by-side garages]({{ '/articles/where-to-find-fleetwood-townhomes-with-true-side-by-side-garages/' | relative_url }}) explains why I would verify the actual garage layout rather than rely on the listing description.

## Bylaws Only Tell Half the Story

Strata bylaws might say something like "visitor parking is for visitors only." Fine. But bylaws don't tell you how that rule actually gets enforced, or if it gets enforced consistently. I've walked through complexes just a few streets apart that looked identical on paper and worked in completely opposite ways, all because of how each community actually managed its stalls.

This is where you do your homework before you buy, not after.

Go see the place at different times. Evenings and weekends tell you more than a Tuesday morning walkthrough ever will. Look around while you're there. Tow-away signs everywhere? A parking area that feels neglected, or one that feels like a police state? Both are signs something's been going on.

Then ask questions. Ask the listing agent or the strata manager, if there is one, how parking gets monitored. Ask if the strata gets a lot of complaints. Ask if there's a waiting list for extra resident parking. You'll learn more from those answers than you will from any one document.

## Check What the Documents Actually Tell You

There are a few different parking questions that can get mixed together during a purchase.

The parking assigned to the home is one question. Visitor parking for the complex is another.

B.C.'s prescribed Form B includes information about parking stalls allocated to a strata lot and can identify whether a stall is part of the strata lot, a separate strata lot, limited common property or common property. The provincial Strata Property Regulation sets out those disclosure fields in [Form B](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/crbc/crbc/12_43_2000_pit_2023_04_01){: target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" }.

That information matters, but it still does not tell you whether the visitor section fills up every weekend.

For that, I would read the strata minutes and parking rules together. Look for repeated complaints, towing discussions, enforcement changes, requests for additional parking or ongoing arguments about residents using visitor stalls.

This is similar to how I approach strata fees. One number rarely tells the whole story. My guide to [Fleetwood townhouse strata fees]({{ '/articles/what-is-a-normal-strata-fee-for-a-fleetwood-townhouse/' | relative_url }}) explains why the documents behind the number matter more than the number by itself.

## Why This Actually Matters

Bad visitor parking doesn't just annoy you once. It wears on you. You end up arguing with neighbours. You worry every time a friend visits that their car might get towed. That kind of stress adds up, and it can turn a home you love into a home you're tired of.

Parking can also be one of the practical differences between older and newer complexes. Age alone does not tell you which will work better, but site layout, garage configuration, density and how the strata operates can all affect daily life. If you're weighing those broader trade-offs, see my comparison of [older Fleetwood townhouses and new builds]({{ '/articles/older-fleetwood-townhouses-vs-new-builds-which-offers-better-value/' | relative_url }}).

Do the extra digging now. It's a lot easier than trying to fix a parking problem after you've already moved in.

  <details>
    <summary>Can the strata council change the parking rules after I move in?</summary>
    <p>Potentially, yes. Under B.C.'s <a href="https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/98043_07" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Strata Property Act</a>, a strata corporation may make rules governing the use, safety and condition of common property and common assets. A new rule generally ceases to have effect at the first annual general meeting after it is made unless it is ratified by majority vote. Bylaw amendments follow a different process. In an entirely residential strata, they generally require approval by a three-quarter vote at an annual or special general meeting and do not take effect until the amendment is filed in the Land Title Office.</p>
  </details>

  <details>
    <summary>Does being close to a future SkyTrain station mean a strata will have visitor parking problems?</summary>
    <p>No. Proximity to a future station does not automatically tell you whether a particular complex will have visitor parking problems. The <a href="https://surreylangleyskytrain.gov.bc.ca/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Surrey Langley SkyTrain project</a> includes 152 Street Station and Fleetwood Station at 160 Street, with the current anticipated in-service date in late 2029. Rather than assuming that location will create a parking problem, check the specific strata's rules, minutes, enforcement history and actual evening and weekend parking conditions.</p>
  </details>

  <details>
    <summary>Is it ever worth buying into a complex with poor visitor parking?</summary>
    <p>Depends on how you live. If guests are rare for you, it might never come up. But if you regularly host family, friends or caregivers, this is one of those details that deserves real weight in your decision. The important thing is knowing about the trade-off before you buy rather than discovering it after you move in.</p>
