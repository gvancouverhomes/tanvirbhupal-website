A street address can be misleading. See "156 Street" on a listing and you might picture a quiet suburban road, the kind where kids ride bikes and play street hockey. Walk the actual street and you'll find something different. 156 Street isn't one road. It changes block by block: how it's built, how much traffic it carries, and how comfortable it feels for a family.

Treating the whole corridor the same way is the biggest mistake an out-of-town buyer makes. Don't go by the listing description. Look at the actual block, how it's laid out, what's missing, and how the traffic moves.

If you are comparing this street with Fleetwood more broadly, my guide to [family safety in Fleetwood]({{ '/articles/is-fleetwood-surrey-safe-for-young-families-to-live/' | relative_url }}) explains why I think the exact property and street matter more than the neighbourhood label.

## The Traffic Profile: 156 Street Does Not Have One Classification

The first thing to understand is that 156 Street does not have one consistent road classification or traffic profile from end to end.

City planning documents identify 156 Street south of 84 Avenue as a collector road in the Fleetwood Enclave area. Around 84 Avenue, Surrey has also described 156 Street as a collector road. Farther north, City development documents identify sections of 156 Street as an arterial road, including around 91 Avenue.

You can see that distinction in Surrey's [Fleetwood Enclave planning document](https://www.surrey.ca/sites/default/files/media/documents/Fleetwood_Enclave__Infill_Area_Plan_Complete_Document.pdf){: target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" } and in individual City planning reports for properties along the corridor.

That matters because collector and arterial roads are intended to carry more than resident-only traffic. Depending on the block, a home on 156 Street can experience a very different day-to-day environment from a house around the corner on an interior local street.

Based on local observation, here's what I would watch for:

- **Commuter flow:** Steady traffic from drivers passing through the neighbourhood, especially during morning drop-off and the afternoon commute.
- **Shortcut traffic:** Drivers may use 156 Street when nearby east-west routes become congested. This can vary by time of day and current construction, so visit during the periods that matter to your family.
- **Mixed traffic:** Cars, buses on some sections, delivery vehicles, turning traffic and vehicles entering or leaving residential driveways.

Given this, the driveway or curb of a home on a busier section of 156 Street is worth treating as a real traffic boundary, not simply an extension of your yard.

If construction disruption is also part of your concern, read [where to buy a house in Fleetwood to reduce construction noise]({{ '/articles/where-to-buy-a-house-in-fleetwood-surrey-to-reduce-construction-noise/' | relative_url }}).

## Infrastructure Continuity: Sidewalks vs. Comfort Buffers

Sidewalk conditions along 156 Street reflect different eras of development, so the pedestrian experience is not identical from one section to another.

Surrey's school travel planning work included a sidewalk and bike lanes on 156 Street between 92 Avenue and 96 Avenue, along with other pedestrian improvements in that area. The City documents those improvements through its [School Travel Planning program](https://www.surrey.ca/services-payments/parking-streets-transportation/vision-zero-surrey/what-we-are-doing/safe-road-users/safe-and-active-schools-program/school-travel-planning){: target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" }.

But the existence of a sidewalk does not automatically tell you how comfortable the walk will feel with a stroller or a young child.

A sidewalk separated from traffic by a grass boulevard, bike lane, parking lane or landscaping can feel very different from one immediately beside moving vehicles. Driveway crossings, intersections, hedges, parked cars and sightlines can also change the experience from one block to the next.

That is why I would walk the exact route instead of looking at the map and simply confirming that a sidewalk exists.

## School Routes Change the Character of the Southern Section

Daily family life near the southern part of 156 Street is heavily influenced by two schools located directly on the corridor.

[Coyote Creek Elementary](https://www.surreyschools.ca/coyotecreek/contact-us){: target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" } is at **8131 156 Street**.

Fleetwood Park Secondary is at **7940 156 Street**, according to the Surrey School District.

Living close to school can be incredibly convenient, but it does not mean the street is automatically quiet during the school day. From what I've seen locally, the area can become noticeably busier around morning drop-off and afternoon dismissal, with parent vehicles, turning movements and children walking through the area.

I would treat that as something to observe rather than as a safety conclusion.

Visit during drop-off.

Come back during dismissal.

Watch where vehicles stop and turn.

Then walk the route your child would actually use.

Move farther north and school catchments change. Depending on the exact address, a home near 156 Street may fall into different elementary catchments. Do not assume the closest school is automatically the school assigned to the property.

Use the official [Surrey Schools School Locator](https://www.surreyschools.ca/demographicsandfacilitiesplanning/school-locator){: target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" } with the exact address before making a housing decision around a school.

## The SkyTrain Factor Near Fraser Highway

The northern part of 156 Street is also affected by a very different kind of change.

The Surrey Langley SkyTrain extension is being built along Fraser Highway, with the project currently anticipating service in **late 2029**. There is no station planned directly at 156 Street. Instead, this part of the corridor sits between **152 Street Station** to the west and **Fleetwood Station at 160 Street** to the east.

The official [Surrey Langley SkyTrain project](https://surreylangleyskytrain.gov.bc.ca/project-overview/){: target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" } provides the current station locations and project timeline.

Provincial transit-oriented development legislation has also established minimum development allowances within defined distances of SkyTrain stations. Surrey has formally designated Transit-Oriented Areas around the future Fleetwood stations, while the broader Fleetwood planning framework continues to be updated to incorporate those provincial requirements.

That distinction matters.

The Transit-Oriented Areas are already established. That does not mean every property near 156 Street and Fraser Highway will immediately redevelop, and it does not mean the final form of every surrounding block is predetermined.

Actual redevelopment still depends on the property, applicable planning rules, site assembly, frontage, servicing, access and other development constraints.

For a family buyer, that creates a practical trade-off. The northern part of 156 Street is close to future rapid transit and an area expected to become more urban over time. Homes farther south are generally farther from the most intense station-area change, although no established residential area should be treated as permanently insulated from future land-use change.

## Hidden Layout Traps for Relocating Families

Parents looking at homes on or near 156 Street can miss small design details that matter more in daily life than they appear to in listing photos.

### Direct Driveway Access onto a Busier Road

A driveway that backs directly onto through-traffic creates a different routine from a driveway on a quiet local street.

Before deciding whether that works for you, stand at the bottom of the driveway and look both ways. Check whether parked vehicles, landscaping, utility equipment or the road alignment limit what you can see.

The question is not whether the driveway is technically usable. It is whether you are comfortable using it every day with the traffic conditions on that exact block.

### The Mouth-of-the-Cul-de-Sac Exposure

Many families prefer the smaller pockets and cul-de-sacs branching off busier roads.

But a home near the entrance can have a different experience from one deep inside the cul-de-sac. Vehicles are still entering from the main road, delivery drivers may turn through, and headlights or turning movements can affect the first few properties differently from homes farther inside.

If that street layout appeals to you, my guide to [why Fleetwood cul-de-sac lots are in demand]({{ '/articles/why-cul-de-sac-lots-in-fleetwood-surrey-command-such-high-demand/' | relative_url }}) goes deeper into the trade-offs.

### Sidewalk Blind Spots and Driveway Density

A sidewalk can look fine in a listing photo but still cross a large number of private driveways.

Hedges, tall fences, mature landscaping, utility boxes and parked vehicles can reduce visibility between a driver leaving a property and a child riding or walking along the sidewalk.

Look at the entire block, not only the frontage of the house you're considering.

### Topography and Sightlines

A small rise, curve or obstruction can reduce how far a driver or pedestrian can see.

Surrey's road-safety work considers sight distance and road conditions when assessing pedestrian infrastructure, but as a buyer you still need to evaluate the actual property yourself.

Stand where your child would cross.

Stand where your car would exit.

Then look at how far you can see in both directions.

### Check Speeds Beyond the School-Zone Boundary

I would also pay attention to what happens immediately beyond the school-zone limits.

Rather than assuming drivers behave a certain way, watch the actual traffic during drop-off and dismissal. Does the pace of traffic change noticeably once vehicles leave the school zone? Are children still walking along those neighbouring blocks?

That observation is more useful than assuming the painted school-zone boundary tells you everything about the surrounding street.

## The Practical Driveway Test for Parents

If you're seriously considering a home on or next to 156 Street, skip relying only on the midday showing.

Come back during the time your family would actually be using the street and run through this five-step check:

1. **Check the sightlines.** Stand at the foot of the driveway. Can you see oncoming traffic clearly in both directions without stepping past a hedge, fence or parked car?
2. **Check the sidewalk buffer.** Is there space between pedestrians and moving vehicles, or does traffic pass immediately beside the sidewalk?
3. **Trace the school route.** Walk the exact route your child would take. Can most of it stay on quieter interior streets, or does it require significant time along a busier section of 156 Street?
4. **Check the turning patterns.** If the home is in a cul-de-sac or side street, watch how vehicles enter and leave from 156 Street and how close the property sits to that movement.
5. **Assess driveway spacing.** Look at neighbouring frontages. Are the sightlines open, or do parked vehicles and landscaping make people on the sidewalk harder to see?

Those five observations will tell you much more about a specific property than the words "quiet street" in a listing.

## Would I Let My Kids Play on 156 Street?

On the main road itself, I would not treat the roadway as an extension of the front yard.

That is different from saying every home on 156 Street is unsuitable for a family.

Some properties have deeper setbacks, fencing, strong pedestrian infrastructure or access to quieter routes behind the home. Others may sit only a few steps from an interior street, park or cul-de-sac.

For independent outdoor play, though, I personally prefer the interior residential streets where children have more separation from through-traffic.

That is one reason the precise location of the house matters so much.

## My Bottom Line

156 Street can be convenient, central and practical, but I would never answer the question by saying the entire road is simply "safe," "unsafe," "quiet" or "busy."

Different sections serve different transportation functions. School activity changes parts of the southern corridor. Pedestrian infrastructure varies. The Fraser Highway end is moving toward a more transit-oriented future. And a house directly on the road can feel very different from a house 100 metres away inside a residential pocket.

So if you're looking at a property here, focus on the block.

Stand in the driveway.

Walk the school route.

Visit during the hours that matter.

Look at the street position.

Then decide whether that exact property gives your family the environment you want.
