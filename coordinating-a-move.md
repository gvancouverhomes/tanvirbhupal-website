# Plan the sale and the purchase as one connected move.

Moving from one home to another is more than deciding what to sell and what to buy. Timing, financing, possession dates, preparation, and the amount of risk your family can comfortably carry all need to work together.

The goal isn't just to close two deals. It's to create enough clarity and flexibility that your family does not have to make the next decision under unnecessary pressure.

## There is no single correct order

Some homeowners do better selling first. Others need to find the right home before they put their current property on the market.

Neither one is automatically safer.

Selling first gives you certainty about your funds. But it can also pressure you to find your next home fast.

Buying first may give you a better chance to secure the right property. But it can bring carrying costs, financing conditions, and uncertainty about the sale.

The right order depends on your finances, your current home, what's available on the market, and how much uncertainty your family can live with.

## Start with the financial structure

Before you choose a direction, you need a clear picture of how the purchase gets funded, and what depends on selling your current home.

That means knowing your equity, your deposit funds, your financing approval, what your lender requires, what you'll likely net from the sale, and whether you can carry both properties for a while.

Sort these questions out early.

A move gets a lot harder when you figure out the financial structure only after the right home shows up, or after an offer is already accepted.

## Understand the risks on both sides

Buying before selling gives you more control over the home you pick. But you lose control over how fast your current home needs to sell.

Selling before buying can give you more certainty about your financial position. But it can leave you house-hunting under a deadline, or arranging a place to stay if the right home isn't ready yet.

Planning isn't about wiping out every risk.

It's about knowing which risks your family can handle, and which ones could push you into a rushed or expensive decision.

  </div>
</section>

## Your current home affects the strategy

How buyers are likely to respond to your current home matters.

Condition, price range, location, how much prep it needs, who's likely to buy it, and what else is competing for buyers, all of that shapes how confidently you can plan the purchase.

A home that needs a lot of work, or takes longer to sell, puts you in a different position than a home you can list right away.

Getting the sale ready early gives you flexibility, even before you've picked a listing date.

## The next home affects it too

The purchase side deserves the same careful look.

Some families want a fairly common type of home, so they've got several options to work with.

Others need something specific, a certain location, schools, accessibility, lot size, room for extended family, parking, or a particular neighbourhood.

The fewer homes that fit, the more carefully you need to plan the order.

Know what you'll compromise on and what you won't. That keeps the timing of your sale from making the buying decision for you.

## Dates can create or reduce pressure

Offer dates, subject removal, completion, possession, your moving arrangements, and when your sale proceeds get released, all of it needs to line up together.

Sometimes both transactions line up closely.

Other times, a longer possession period, a temporary overlap, or a short-term rental makes for a safer transition.

The best plan isn't always the one with the fewest days between homes.

It's the one that gives your family enough certainty and time to move without extra pressure.

  </div>
</section>

## Plan for what may not go perfectly

Your current home may take longer to sell than expected.

The right home to move into may not show up right away.

Financing conditions may change.

Inspection findings, competing offers, or possession dates can force the plan to change.

A good strategy leaves room for all of that, instead of counting on every step happening exactly on time.

That might mean having backup timing options, knowing your carrying costs, planning for temporary housing, or deciding ahead of time what would make you pause.
